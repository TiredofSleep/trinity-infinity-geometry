## 2. The lens on one square

![Dürer's magic square of 1514, numbered from 0 to 15. Its centre value 7.5 is held by no cell; the flip n to 15 − n swaps the low numbers 0 to 7 with the high numbers 8 to 15; a number and its flip always average 7.5; and each pair sits mirrored through the middle of the square.](figures/the_lens.svg)

Take the magic square in Albrecht Dürer's engraving *Melencolia I* (1514), with its numbers counted from
0. It holds 0 to 15, and every row, column and diagonal adds to 30.

1. **The centre you measure from** is 7.5, halfway from 0 to 15. No cell holds it.
2. **The flip** is n → 15 − n; done twice, it changes nothing. Its **two sides** are the low numbers, 0 to
   7, and the high numbers, 8 to 15.
3. **The edge** is what the flip keeps: 7.5 alone, the one number the square is missing.
4. **The glue.** A number and its flip always average 7.5. Every pair measures the centre, and no cell
   stands on it. In Dürer's square each pair also sits mirrored through the middle.

**What it pays for.** That mirror symmetry forces a zero: the square is singular, and its determinant is 0
[FORCED; NAMED: every associative magic square of even order is singular, Mattingly 2000]. Measured from
their missing centre, a square's hard questions become counting questions. That is the opening of
[*A Ledger of Nothing*](towers/kernel_ledger/A_LEDGER_OF_NOTHING.md). The same moves, on the odd magic
squares MATLAB builds, gave a closed form for their determinant (§4).

## 3. Use it on a question of your own [READING]

The parts fit together as six questions, each with an answer that can be checked.

1. **What is everything measured from?** That is the centre. It is usually not one of the things measured.
2. **What move, done twice, changes nothing?** That is the flip. What it swaps are its two sides.
3. **What does the flip keep?** That is the edge. If nothing in your world is kept, the edge is missing,
   and the paradox lives there: √2 for the fractions, *i* for the real numbers.
4. **What do a thing and its flip make together?** Average them, or multiply them: the result lies on the
   edge (§5).
5. **What pays for each zero?** A count you can make, or a coincidence in the numbers
   ([*A Ledger of Nothing*](towers/kernel_ledger/A_LEDGER_OF_NOTHING.md)).
6. **What did not survive?** Keep both sides rather than choosing one, and keep each dead end with the
   evidence that killed it ([`GRAVEYARD.md`](GRAVEYARD.md)).

## 5. The glue: a thing and its flip land on the edge [FORCED]

Take a flip σ on things that can be averaged or multiplied.
- **The average.** If σ keeps midpoints, as every reflection does, then the average of *x* and σ(*x*) is
  kept by σ: it lies on the edge.
- **The product.** If σ respects products and their order does not matter, or if σ reverses the order of
  products, then σ(*x* · σ(*x*)) = *x* · σ(*x*): the product lies on the edge.

| the coin | the flip | a thing and its flip, together | where it lands |
|---|---|---|---|
| a magic square's numbers, 0 to 15 | *n* → 15 − *n* | the average: always 7.5 | on the edge, which no cell holds |
| real and imaginary | *z* → *z̄* | the average, Re *z*; the product, *z z̄* = \|*z*\|² | on the reals; the product is never below 0 |
| the two square roots of 2 | *p* + *q*√2 → *p* − *q*√2 | the product, *p*² − 2*q*² | on the whole numbers; it is 0 only when *p* = *q* = 0, because no fraction squares to 2 |
| a quaternion and its conjugate (order reversed) | *q* → *q̄* | the product, *q q̄* = \|*q*\|² | on the reals, although quaternions do not commute |
| a matrix and its transpose (order reversed) | *A* → *A*ᵀ | the average, (*A* + *A*ᵀ)/2; the product, *A A*ᵀ | on the symmetric matrices; *A A*ᵀ has no negative eigenvalue |
| a body and its polar | *K* → *K*° | the product of their volumes, \|*K*\| · \|*K*°\| | one number for both sides; largest at the round ball [NAMED: Blaschke and Santaló] |

- [NAMED] The average is the edge part of the coin's split into an edge part and a side part; the product
  is the norm, and for matrices the Gram matrix.
- [READING] The author's *"a quadratic operator if duality is given duality"* (March 2026) names the
  product: a duality applied to a duality, which makes a square.
- Checked by [`coin/verify_lens.py`](coin/verify_lens.py) (23 checks, including §2's square); the polar
  pair is checked in full by [`coin/verify_polar_coin.py`](coin/verify_polar_coin.py).

## 7. A prediction, registered before any test

A lens should predict as well as describe. This one predicts with its coin.

- [COIN] **The pattern so far.** On the sphere, the octahedron wins every energy contest and its polar,
  the cube, loses every one. The tetrahedron, its own polar and so on the coin's edge, wins every one
  ([`base/SYMMETRY_AND_ENERGY.md`](base/SYMMETRY_AND_ENERGY.md)). The next polar pair is the icosahedron
  and the dodecahedron, and the icosahedron wins every contest [NAMED: Cohn and Kumar 2007].
- **The prediction.** The dodecahedron loses every contest: for every completely monotone potential, some
  20 points on the sphere have lower energy than its 20 corners.
- **Why it could fail.**
  - The cube's proof does not carry over directly. The cube's 45° turn takes two facing squares from
    aligned to staggered, and the dodecahedron's two middle pentagons are staggered already.
  - The dodecahedron balances every polynomial up to degree 5 (a 5-design). As a potential grows weaker,
    it rewards balance first, so the weakest contests are where it could hold.
  - One contest is settled already: under Coulomb's law the best known 20 points are not the dodecahedron
    [NAMED: the published Thomson minima; the run will recompute it].
- **The run.** The seven contests of
  [`base/verify_symmetry_and_energy.py`](base/verify_symmetry_and_energy.py): powers *s* = 1, 3, 12 and 40,
  Gaussians *a* = 1 and 4, and the logarithm. In each:
  - the dodecahedron's energy;
  - the lowest energy found from 200 random starts (in the two steepest contests each start is first spread
    out under Coulomb's law), and from 20 more starts that nudge the dodecahedron itself;
  - whether the dodecahedron is a saddle: whether some small move lowers its energy, once its three turns
    are set aside.
- **Kill condition.** The prediction dies if, in any of the seven contests, nothing below the dodecahedron
  is found. It is proved only by an argument that covers every completely monotone potential, as the
  cube's twist does.
- **What it tests.** If it holds, the coin's pattern holds for both polar pairs: one side wins every
  contest, the other loses every one, and the edge wins. If it dies, the pattern belonged to the
  octahedron and the cube alone, and it goes to the graveyard with the run that killed it.
- **Status.** Registered with this page on 2026-11-07, before any run. The result will be scored here; the
  literature gate comes after the run.

### The result (run on 2026-11-07, after the page was committed)

**The prediction held.** The dodecahedron loses all seven contests
([`base/run_dodecahedron.py`](base/run_dodecahedron.py); its log is
[`base/run_dodecahedron.log`](base/run_dodecahedron.log)).

| contest | the dodecahedron | the lowest found |
|---|---|---|
| Coulomb, 1/*r* | 151.7986206 | 150.8815683, Thomson's published minimum |
| 1/*r*³ | 141.1416483 | 131.8130144 |
| 1/*r*¹² | 1730.120468 | 616.4349645 |
| 1/*r*⁴⁰ | 21,769,684.57 | 262,608.9318 |
| Gaussian, *a* = 1 | 39.11006491 | 39.08824527 |
| Gaussian, *a* = 4 | 4.203089141 | 3.572849905 |
| logarithm | −53.61727374 | −54.01112997 |

In every contest the dodecahedron is also a saddle: one family of three moves lowers its energy.

- **Known in part** [NAMED]. That the dodecahedron is not universally optimal was known:
  - Cohn and Kumar (2007) note it is not even an optimal spherical code;
  - Ballinger et al. (2009) find it loses the harmonic contest;
  - Nerattini, Brauchart and Kiessling (2014) state that it minimizes no power law with *s* > −2, which
    covers five of the seven contests.

  The run adds the two Gaussians and the saddle.
- **Rigid wins, floppy loses** [FORCED, measured; found by the run, not part of the prediction]. Put bars
  along each solid's edges and keep its corners on the sphere.
  - The three solids with triangle faces cannot flex, and each is a local minimum in all seven contests.
  - The cube can flex in 2 ways and the dodecahedron in 8, and each is a saddle in all seven.
  - The cube's twist is one of its flexes, and the dodecahedron's three losing moves are flexes too.
- [READING] **The coin meets the ledger.** Those flexes are zeros of the kind
  [*A Ledger of Nothing*](towers/kernel_ledger/A_LEDGER_OF_NOTHING.md) counts: the floppy modes of a frame,
  paid for by a count. Moving the corners on the sphere, less the three turns, gives the cube 13 freedoms
  for its 12 bars, and the dodecahedron 37 for its 30, so at least 1 flex and at least 7 are forced. The
  triangle-faced solids have more bars than freedoms, and none of them can flex. On the polar coin, the
  side that wins is the braced one, and the side that loses is the one the count leaves floppy.
- **Then proved: the dodecahedron loses every contest** [FORCED: exact arithmetic in ℚ(√5),
  [`base/verify_dodecahedron.py`](base/verify_dodecahedron.py); NEW: not found in the literature we checked].
  - Every potential completely monotone on (0, 4] is a sum Σ *c*ₙ (4 − *r*²)ⁿ with every *c*ₙ ≥ 0
    (Bernstein). Along one of the floppy moves, the second derivative of the energy is Σ *c*ₙ 2ⁿ Λₙ.
  - Λₙ = 0 for *n* ≤ 5, and Λₙ < 0 for every *n* ≥ 6: exactly up to 60, and by an explicit bound beyond.
    The first of them is Λ₆ = −320/81.
  - So the prediction is a theorem for every contest in this page's sense, the mixtures of Gaussians.
  - Cohn and Kumar's slightly larger class has one exception, and it is the coin's own edge. A polynomial of
    degree at most 5 in *r*² cannot tell the dodecahedron from the best, because it is a 5-design, so there
    it ties. The registered "why it could fail" pointed exactly there: balance up to degree 5.
  - The same computation one step down gives the cube. It ties every contest that stops at degree 3, its
    design strength, and loses all the rest.

## 8. What this page is, and is not

- **Is:** the author's lens in the author's words, each part next to what it became and how firmly that
  stands. It maps the person to the results, so that a reader can judge both.
- **Is not:** a claim that the lens proves anything by itself. The theorems stand on their proofs and
  their scripts. The lens is how they were found, and where to look next. Physics stays behind the fence.
